The October Comment Files: Who Has Actually Filed

Treasury has 26 comments. The SEC has 30 entries, two of which are memoranda recording meetings with staff rather than letters from the public. Both files are open until October.

By Kansas Prairie Webworks · September 17, 2026 · 5 min read
Educational technology commentary. Not investment, legal, tax, or financial advice.

Read aloud by your browser. Nothing is downloaded and nothing is sent anywhere.

Sources & verification (6)

The short version

Two federal rulemakings that would set the terms for digital dollars and crypto asset offerings close their comment periods in October. With five weeks left, the public record is thin. Treasury's docket, TREAS-DO-2026-0496, holds 26 comments posted between August 27 and September 11. The SEC's file for Regulation Crypto Assets, S7-2026-27, holds 30 entries dated August 25 to September 16, plus a bundle of seven identical form letters.

Read the names and the shape of it changes. Most filings are from individuals, several are anonymous, and the organisations that do appear are mostly small: a general counsel at Ava Labs, founders at Ohanae, American CryptoFed, Immutifi, TrustLogic. As of September 17 neither file contains a letter from a national banking or securities trade association.

The part that is easy to miss is that two of the SEC's thirty entries are not comments. They are memoranda from the Division of Corporation Finance recording meetings with Coinbase and with Digital Asset, Inc. The largest firm in the file did not write a letter. It took a meeting, and the meeting is in the public record because the rules require it to be. That is the part of a comment file most people never read.

What is in the two files today

Treasury's docket covers section 3 of the GENIUS Act, the rules for who may issue a payment stablecoin and on what terms. Counted through the government's own docket interface on September 17, it holds 26 comments, posted between August 27 and September 11. Sixteen of those arrived in the fifteen days to September 17 and one arrived in the last seven. The file has gone quiet.

The SEC's file for Regulation Crypto Assets holds 30 entries dated August 25 to September 16: 28 public comments and two meeting memoranda. Alongside them the Commission lists a Type A bundle, its label for identical form letters submitted by more than one person, carrying seven signatures.

The filers are not who a reader would guess from the coverage. Three comments are anonymous. Several are from named individuals with no affiliation. The organisations that appear are small and mostly crypto-native: Ava Labs, through its general counsel; Ohanae, Inc. and Ohanae Securities; American CryptoFed; Adamson Brothers; JK Advisory; Immutifi; Beast Industries; TrustLogic; the Sovereign Stack Initiative. One is a doctoral candidate at Ariel University.

Fifty six entries, between the two largest digital asset rulemakings of the year, in the first five weeks, and two of them are records of meetings rather than filings. A local zoning dispute can draw more.

The meetings are in the file, and they are the part to read

On August 26 the SEC's Division of Corporation Finance met Digital Asset, Inc. On September 2 it met Coinbase. Both meetings are in the comment file as memoranda, posted August 27 and September 2, because a rulemaking record is supposed to capture the arguments an agency hears privately as well as the ones it receives by post.

This is the mechanism worth understanding. A comment letter is a document anyone can send. A meeting memorandum is the agency's own note that a conversation happened, who was in it, and what was discussed. The letters tell you who wants to be on the record. The memoranda tell you who got in the room.

Coinbase is the most consequential name in either file, and it does not appear as a letter writer. A reader counting comment letters to gauge industry opinion would miss it completely. Anyone reading a rulemaking file should read the meeting log first and the letters second, which is the reverse of how these files are usually summarised.

What happens between now and the deadlines

Treasury's comments close October 19, 2026 and the SEC's close October 20, 2026. Both dates are in the Federal Register notices, and both files remain open until then.

Comment files in rulemakings of this size are usually back-loaded. The considered institutional letters, the ones with counsel's name on them and an appendix of legal argument, tend to arrive in the final days rather than the first weeks, because the organisations writing them are reading the proposal, polling members and clearing text internally. A thin file five weeks out is normal, and it is not evidence that nobody cares. What it does mean is that anyone describing industry consensus today is describing something that has not been filed yet.

The Senate's September 15 refusal to take up the CLARITY Act sharpened this. That bill would have written the division of authority these two agencies are currently exercising. It did not advance, so these dockets are now the live process, and the record closing in October is the record that a final rule will be built on.

One practical note, because it is genuinely open to anyone: both files accept comments from the public, a filing becomes part of the permanent record, and the agency must consider what it receives. A business with a concrete operational objection, the kind a bank or a processor would not think to raise, is allowed to put it in writing before the deadline.

The letters tell you who wants to be on the record. The memoranda tell you who got in the room.

Fifty six entries, five weeks out, and the biggest name in either file never wrote a letter. The comment record that a final rule will rest on is still mostly empty, and the institutional letters that will fill it have not been written yet. Anyone citing what the industry has told regulators is, for the moment, citing two dozen individuals and a meeting log.

Why Kansas Prairie Webworks is paying attention

I read comment files for the same reason I read cloture motions. They are public, they are free, and they are the actual input to a decision that vendors will later describe to you in summary form. The gap between the summary and the file is usually where the selling happens.

For a business here the useful version is short. When a processor or a platform tells you the rules are settled, or that the industry has agreed on an approach, these two dockets are where that claim can be checked. Today they contain 56 filings and two meetings. That is not a consensus. It is a file that is still open, and it stays open until the third week of October.

Questions people are asking

What are the two comment periods closing in October 2026?

Treasury's proposed rule on payment stablecoin issuance, offer and sale under section 3 of the GENIUS Act, docket TREAS-DO-2026-0496, closes October 19, 2026. The SEC's Regulation Crypto Assets, File No. S7-2026-27, closes October 20, 2026. Both are proposed rules, which means each agency has published a draft and asked the public what is wrong with it.

How many comments have been filed so far?

Counted on September 17, 2026, Treasury's docket held 26 comments posted between August 27 and September 11, and the SEC's file held 30 entries dated August 25 to September 16, of which 28 are public comments and two are meeting memoranda. The SEC also lists a Type A bundle of seven identical form letters.

What is a meeting memorandum, and why is it in a comment file?

It is the agency's own written record that staff met someone outside the agency about the rulemaking, naming the participants and the subject. It sits in the public comment file so the record shows the arguments an agency heard in person, not only the ones sent in writing. In this file two such memoranda record meetings with Digital Asset, Inc. on August 26 and Coinbase on September 2, 2026.

Can a small business file a comment?

Yes. Both dockets accept submissions from anyone, and a filing becomes part of the permanent public record that the agency must consider before adopting a final rule. There is no requirement to be represented by counsel or to address the whole proposal. A comment describing one concrete operational consequence is a normal and useful filing.

Does a comment period closing mean the rules take effect?

No. A comment deadline is the last day the proposal is still an argument. After it passes the agency reads what it received and may adopt the rule as proposed, adopt it changed, re-propose it, or leave it. No statutory clock forces a final rule to appear at any particular point afterwards.

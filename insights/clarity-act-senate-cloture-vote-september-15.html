Cloture Rejected: The CLARITY Act Vote, 49 to 50

The Senate voted on whether it could begin debating the CLARITY Act and said no. Four Republicans voted against, no Democrat voted in favour, and the bill kept its place on the calendar.

By Kansas Prairie Webworks · September 9, 2026 · 6 min read · updated September 17, 2026
Educational technology commentary. Not investment, legal, tax, or financial advice.

The short version

At 2:19 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday, September 15, 2026, the Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633, the Digital Asset Market CLARITY Act. The roll call was 49 yeas to 50 nays, with one senator not voting, against a threshold of three-fifths of all senators duly chosen and sworn. That is Roll Call Vote 234, and the Senate records the result as "Cloture on the Motion to Proceed Rejected".

The arithmetic is the story. Forty nine yeas were all Republican. Four Republicans voted no: Collins of Maine, Hawley of Missouri, Moran of Kansas and Tillis of North Carolina. Not one Democrat or independent voted yes. A measure eleven votes short, with none of the missing eleven coming from the other side of the aisle, did not fail narrowly on the day. It failed on a question that was never close.

What did not change is the part worth acting on. The measure kept its place on the Senate Calendar as Order No. 423, and no further cloture motion had been filed as of September 17. Meanwhile the two federal comment periods written under the current division of authority are still open and still close in October. The vote that was reported as deciding the future of digital asset regulation decided which chamber was not going to write it this month.

What changed since publication

What the Senate actually did at 2:19 on Tuesday

The question put to the chamber, in the Senate's own words on the roll call, was "On Cloture on the Motion to Proceed H.R. 3633", recorded under the vote title "Motion to Invoke Cloture: Motion to Proceed to H.R. 3633". Cloture is the procedure that ends debate. Since 1975 it has required three-fifths of all senators duly chosen and sworn, which the Senate's own explainer states plainly as 60 of the 100-member Senate.

The tally was 49 yeas, 50 nays, one not voting. Because the threshold counts senators chosen and sworn rather than senators present, the absence changed nothing: 60 was the number either way.

The per-senator record is worth reading rather than summarising, because summaries of this vote disagreed with each other on the day. Every one of the 49 yeas was Republican. Four Republicans voted nay: Collins of Maine, Hawley of Missouri, Moran of Kansas and Tillis of North Carolina. The 50 nays were those four, 44 Democrats and both independents. Coons of Delaware did not vote.

Kansas readers can note that their senior senator was one of the four. His office had published no statement on the vote as of September 17, and this brief does not guess at a reason the record does not supply. What the record does show is the timing. On September 14, the day before the vote, three of the bill's Senate sponsors released what they called the final text, and their announcement led with a Treasury circuit-breaker against deposit flight, giving the Secretary authority they said would "protect community banks and the farmers and small businesses who rely on them". That argument was published to farm-state senators one day before the roll call. It did not carry all of them.

The House settled its position fourteen months ago, on July 17, 2025, passing H.R. 3633 by 294 to 134 on Roll Call 199. Tuesday was the first time the Senate voted on the measure at all, and what it voted on was whether to start.

The agencies kept writing while the Senate stopped

Two federal comment periods on digital asset rules close about a month from now, and both were drafted inside the allocation of authority H.R. 3633 would have rewritten.

Treasury's proposed rule on payment stablecoin issuance, offer and sale was published in the Federal Register on August 18, 2026, docket TREAS-DO-2026-0496. Comments close October 19, 2026. The SEC's Regulation Crypto Assets was published on August 21, 2026, File No. S7-2026-27. Comments close October 20, 2026. Both carry the Federal Register document type "Proposed Rule".

Before Tuesday, the awkwardness was that two agencies were asking the public what was wrong with rules written under a division of authority the Senate might be in the middle of replacing. After Tuesday, that awkwardness is simply gone. The Senate declined to take up the replacement, so the proposals open for comment are the only live process in which the rules that govern this are being written. The comment deadlines did not move, and they now matter more than they did last week rather than less.

This is the inversion most of the coverage missed. A failed vote on a market structure bill reads as nothing happening. What it actually did was leave the existing framework, and the rulemaking being conducted under it, as the only game in town for the rest of the year.

What the rejection forecloses, and what it does not

A rejected cloture motion on a motion to proceed does not strike a bill from the calendar. H.R. 3633 still sits as Order No. 423, and the Calendar of Business for September 16 still carries it under that heading, along with the unanimous consent agreement from Tuesday waiving the mandatory quorum call under Rule XXII. The Senate's list of cloture motions for the 119th Congress shows one entry for the measure, filed August 8 and disposed of on September 15 with the result 49-50.

What the vote forecloses is the current path. Until the Senate agrees to take the bill up, none of the rest can begin: no floor debate on the text, no amendments, no vote on passage, and no reconciliation with the House-passed version. Every one of those steps was still ahead of the bill before Tuesday, and all of them remain ahead of it now.

What it does not foreclose is the subject. The bill exists, the House has passed it, the committee text is written, and nothing in Tuesday's result repealed any of that. Predicting what the Senate does next is not this brief's business, and anyone offering you a date should be asked which document it comes from.

For a business the practical position is unchanged and now unambiguous. The framework governing digital assets is the one currently in force. The rules being written under it are open for comment until October 19 and October 20. There is no new statute to prepare for, and there was no new statute last Tuesday either.

The vote did not fail by one. It failed by eleven, and by the eleven it never had.

The Senate declined to begin. That is the whole of what happened on September 15. The framework governing digital assets is still the one in force, the two proposals written under it are still open for comment until October 19 and October 20, and the bill still sits on the calendar as Order No. 423. Anyone telling you the law changed on Tuesday is describing a motion the Senate refused to take up.

Why Kansas Prairie Webworks is paying attention

I read cloture motions for the same reason I read Federal Register dockets, which is that they are short, free, and say exactly what happened, while the coverage built on top of them frequently does not. When this brief was first published, the result field on that Senate entry was blank. It now reads 49-50, and the entry is still a better account of where the bill stands than most of what was written about it on the day.

For a business in Kansas the practical version is narrow. If a payment processor, bank, custodian or software vendor tells you something is newly permitted because of the CLARITY Act, they are describing a bill that has passed one chamber and that the other chamber has declined to take up. The framework that actually governs their product is the one in force now, plus two proposals that are still taking comment until October. That is worth knowing before signing anything that assumes otherwise.

Questions people are asking

What happened to the CLARITY Act on September 15, 2026?

The Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633 by 49 yeas to 50 nays, with one senator not voting. Cloture is the procedure that ends debate and requires three-fifths of all senators duly chosen and sworn, which is 60 in a 100-member Senate. Because the Senate must first agree to take up a bill sitting on its calendar, the vote was about whether the Senate could begin considering the measure. It was not a vote on the bill's text and not a vote on passage.

Has the CLARITY Act passed?

No. The House passed H.R. 3633 on July 17, 2025 by 294 to 134 on Roll Call 199. The Senate has not passed it and, as of September 15, 2026, has not agreed to take it up. A bill becomes law when it has passed both chambers in identical form and been signed.

Who voted against it?

All 50 nays were four Republicans, 44 Democrats and both independents. The four Republicans were Collins of Maine, Hawley of Missouri, Moran of Kansas and Tillis of North Carolina. Every one of the 49 yeas was Republican, and Coons of Delaware did not vote. The per-senator record is published by the Senate as Roll Call Vote 234.

Is the bill dead?

The vote did not remove it. H.R. 3633 remains on the Senate Calendar as Order No. 423, and the Calendar of Business for September 16, 2026 still lists it there. What the rejection does is block the current path: until the Senate agrees to take the bill up, there is no floor debate on the text, no amendments and no vote on passage. As of September 17, 2026 the Senate's cloture list showed no further motion filed on the measure.

Does the failed vote change the SEC and Treasury proposals open for comment?

No. Treasury's payment stablecoin proposal closes for comment on October 19, 2026 and the SEC's Regulation Crypto Assets closes October 20, 2026. Both remain proposed rules under the authority the agencies hold today, and that authority is exactly what the Senate declined to rewrite. If anything the deadlines matter more now, because the rulemaking is the only live process in which these rules are being written.

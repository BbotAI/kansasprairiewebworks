The Comment Files Closed. Here Is Who Filed

In September both files were thin and no national trade association had written. They closed in October with the banks, the state regulators and the largest crypto firms all on the record, most of them in the final days.

By Kansas Prairie Webworks · November 24, 2026 · 5 min read
Educational technology commentary. Not investment, legal, tax, or financial advice.

Read aloud by your browser. Nothing is downloaded and nothing is sent anywhere.

Sources & verification (7)

The short version

Two federal digital asset rulemakings closed for comment in October. Treasury's docket for the GENIUS Act stablecoin rule, TREAS-DO-2026-0496, now holds 162 comments, and 84 of them were posted on a single day, October 20. The SEC's file for Regulation Crypto Assets, S7-2026-27, lists 256 entries, and half of them are dated in the final week.

The names changed with the numbers. In September neither file carried a letter from a national banking or securities trade association. The closed files hold the American Bankers Association, the Bank Policy Institute, the Independent Community Bankers of America and SIFMA, alongside Coinbase, Circle, Stripe and Mastercard, and on the SEC side the New York Attorney General, the Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth and the state securities regulators' association.

The part that cuts against the usual reading is that a closed comment period is not a closed record. Both agencies kept meeting outsiders after their deadlines and posting the memoranda, and letters dated after the deadline keep appearing in the SEC file. Four days after Treasury's file closed, a different regulator, the OCC, published the first final GENIUS Act rule. Treasury's own rule is still a proposal.

What the two files hold now

Treasury's docket covers section 3 of the GENIUS Act, the rules on who may issue, offer and sell a payment stablecoin in the United States. Counted through the government's own docket interface on November 24, it holds 162 comments. On September 17, five weeks before the deadline, it held 26. Of the 162, 84 were posted on October 20, the day after comments closed on October 19, and one more was posted on November 1.

The SEC's file lists 256 entries on November 24: 247 public comments, eight memoranda of meetings with SEC officials, and one staff study. Separately it lists two bundles of identical form letters, Type A with 8 signatures and Type B with 5. The SEC dates each entry by the date on the letter rather than the day it was posted, and 128 of the 256, exactly half, are dated October 14 through October 20, the final week. Seventy eight are dated October 20 itself.

That is the shape the September files predicted. Institutional letters are slow to write and arrive at the end, and a file that looks empty five weeks out is a file whose authors are still clearing text with counsel.

The names that arrived at the deadline

The banks came in on the last day. Treasury's October 20 postings include the American Bankers Association, the Bank Policy Institute, the Independent Community Bankers of America, America's Credit Unions, the Independent Bankers Association of Texas, the Institute of International Bankers and SIFMA, plus a joint letter from the Conference of State Bank Supervisors, the Money Transmitter Regulators Association, NASCUS and the National Conference of State Legislatures. The same day's postings carry the payments and crypto firms the rule is written for: Coinbase, Circle, Stripe, Mastercard, Ripple, Anchorage Digital Bank and Andreessen Horowitz. The New York State Department of Financial Services had already filed, posted October 5, and Representative Warren Davidson's letter was posted October 15.

The SEC file gathered a different crowd for a different rule. SIFMA, the Investment Company Institute, the ICBA and Nasdaq wrote. So did three state officials: New York Attorney General Letitia James, Massachusetts Secretary of the Commonwealth William Galvin and California's Department of Financial Protection and Innovation, together with the North American Securities Administrators Association. Investor advocates filed too, including Better Markets, Public Citizen, Americans for Financial Reform Education Fund and the American Association for Justice, and John Coates, John C. Coffee, Jr., James D. Cox, Merritt B. Fox and Joel Seligman sent a joint letter dated October 7.

Coinbase is the useful marker. In September it appeared in the SEC file only as a meeting memorandum. It now has a letter in both files. The considered positions of the largest participants were simply not written yet when the early summaries of industry opinion were.

A closed file is not a finished record

The deadlines did not end the conversations. Treasury posted a memorandum of a November 2 meeting with the New York Department of Financial Services on November 9, two weeks after its comment period closed. The SEC's Division of Economic and Risk Analysis met finance professor Andreas Park on November 2, and the Division of Corporation Finance met him again on November 4. Both memoranda are in the file. Letters dated October 27, October 28, November 2, November 18 and November 20 have been added to the SEC list as well.

Meanwhile the first GENIUS Act rule to become final came from a different regulator. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency published its final rule for the stablecoin issuers it supervises on October 23, effective January 18, 2027. It reports receiving about 328 comments on its own proposal. The same document states that the prohibitions in section 3 of the Act are outside its scope and that Treasury has proposed rules to address them. Treasury's section 3 rule has not been finalized, and the SEC's proposal has no deadline for a final rule at all.

So the practical sequence is uneven. One set of GENIUS Act rules is final for one regulator's institutions. The Treasury rule that applies more broadly is still being decided, from a record that kept growing after it closed. Anyone describing the stablecoin rules as settled is describing the OCC's piece of them.

The comment period closed. The meetings did not.

The record that was missing in September showed up on deadline day, and it has not stopped arriving. Most of what will shape the final stablecoin and crypto offering rules was filed in a single week, the agencies were still taking meetings in November, and the only GENIUS Act rule that is actually final belongs to one bank regulator.

Why Kansas Prairie Webworks is paying attention

I count these files for the same reason I counted them in September. Vendors will summarize what the industry told regulators, and the summary is easier to sell than the file. The file is free and it is specific about who said what and when.

For a business here the useful version is short. If a processor or platform tells you the stablecoin rules are done, ask which regulator's rules they mean. One is final and takes effect January 18, 2027. Treasury's broader rule is still a proposal, and both agencies are still meeting people about what goes into theirs.

Questions people are asking

How many comments did the two October rulemakings receive?

Counted on November 24, 2026, Treasury's GENIUS Act docket TREAS-DO-2026-0496 held 162 comments and the SEC's Regulation Crypto Assets file S7-2026-27 listed 256 entries: 247 public comments, eight memoranda of meetings with SEC officials and one staff study. The SEC also lists two form-letter bundles, Type A with 8 signatures and Type B with 5.

Did the national trade associations file?

Yes, at the end. In September neither file carried a letter from a national banking or securities trade association. Treasury's October 20 postings include the American Bankers Association, the Bank Policy Institute, the Independent Community Bankers of America and SIFMA. SIFMA, the Investment Company Institute and the ICBA also wrote to the SEC.

Can an agency still take input after the comment period closes?

It does, and the record shows it. Treasury posted a memorandum of a November 2, 2026 meeting with the New York Department of Financial Services, and the SEC posted memoranda of meetings with finance professor Andreas Park on November 2 and November 4. Letters dated after the deadline have also been added to the SEC list.

Are the GENIUS Act stablecoin rules final?

One set is. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency published a final rule on October 23, 2026 for payment stablecoin issuers under its jurisdiction, effective January 18, 2027. Treasury's rule under section 3 of the Act was still a proposed rule as of November 24, 2026.

When will the SEC finalize Regulation Crypto Assets?

There is no set date. Closing the comment period starts the agency's review of the record. It may adopt the rule as proposed, adopt it with changes, re-propose it or leave it, and no statutory clock requires a final rule by any particular day.

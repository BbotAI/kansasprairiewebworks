Who Moved Money On Chain, and What the Filings Required

A Form D amended on October 6, 2026 shows a Franklin Templeton onchain fund claiming the qualified purchaser exclusion, accepting no non-accredited investors, and reporting a five million dollar minimum. Meanwhile the largest market maker in the United States has told the SEC to slow down.

By Kansas Prairie Webworks · October 8, 2026 · 9 min read
Educational technology commentary. Not investment, legal, tax, or financial advice.

Read aloud by your browser. Nothing is downloaded and nothing is sent anywhere.

Sources & verification (8)

The short version

Three of the largest asset managers in the world now run tokenized cash funds on public blockchains. BlackRock's BUIDL is live on nine networks. Franklin Templeton's BENJI represents shares of a U.S.-registered money market fund on nine more. JPMorgan reports roughly $7 billion transacted daily through its Kinexys unit.

The interesting documents are not the announcements. They are the exemption filings underneath. A Form D for Franklin Onchain Institutional Liquidity Fund Ltd., amended on October 6, 2026, claims Rule 506(b) and Investment Company Act section 3(c)(7), the exclusion available only to funds whose investors are all qualified purchasers. It records a minimum investment of $5,000,000 and no non-accredited investors.

And the dissent is already on file. On December 2, 2025 Citadel Securities asked the SEC to refrain from granting broad exemptive relief for tokenized equities, warning it would create "two separate regulatory regimes for the trading of the same security".

What the exemptions actually say

Start with a document almost nobody has read, because it was filed two days before this brief and nothing has been written about it.

Search EDGAR, the SEC's filing system, for the word "onchain" and one registrant comes back: Franklin Onchain Institutional Liquidity Fund Ltd., CIK 0002068319, a Delaware corporation incorporated in 2024. It has made exactly two filings. A Notice of Exempt Offering of Securities in May 2025, and an amendment to it on October 6, 2026.

That notice is a Form D, which is what an issuer files when it sells securities without registering them publicly. The machine-readable version of the filing lists three federal exemptions by code: 06b, 3C and 3C.7.

Those codes do the work. 06b is Rule 506(b), the private placement safe harbour. 3C.7 is section 3(c)(7) of the Investment Company Act, and the statute is worth reading in the original, because it is three lines long and it settles the question of who is allowed in:

"Any issuer, the outstanding securities of which are owned exclusively by persons who, at the time of acquisition of such securities, are qualified purchasers, and which is not making and does not at that time propose to make a public offering of such securities."

A qualified purchaser is a higher bar than an accredited investor. The rest of the filing is consistent with it. The offering amount is recorded as Indefinite. The industry group is Pooled Investment Fund. The answer to whether there are non-accredited investors is false. And the minimum investment accepted is $5,000,000.

Two fields deserve to be quoted exactly rather than summarised, because they are easy to misread in the direction of a better story. Total amount sold is reported as 0, and the number of investors who have already invested is reported as 0. A dashboard elsewhere lists a Franklin institutional token holding well over a billion dollars, and the names are similar enough that the obvious move is to treat this filing as that fund's paperwork. The filing does not support it. Whatever that money is, this document reports no sales.

One more detail, reported rather than explained. The amendment was filed on October 6, 2026 and signed on May 28, 2026, by Matthew Hinkle. Form D requires an annual amendment, so a May signature is consistent with a routine annual filing. The gap between the two dates is in the record and this brief does not account for it.

What they are saying, including the firm saying slow down

The public statements are easier to find and say less.

On August 3, 2026 BlackRock announced two tokenized cash products: OnChain Shares of the BlackRock Select Treasury Based Liquidity Fund (BSTBL), and the BlackRock Daily Reinvestment Stablecoin Reserve Vehicle (BRSRV). Both on Ethereum. The detail in that release worth keeping is that they use different transfer agents: BNY for BSTBL, Securitize for BRSRV. A transfer agent is the entity that keeps the official record of who owns what, and running two of them across two products tells you the firm is not treating tokenization as one settled technology choice.

The release also supplies its own scale check, which is useful because it comes from one document with consistent dating. U.S. money market funds hold more than $8.4 trillion as of May 2026. BlackRock's own cash management group oversees nearly $1.073 trillion as of March 2026. Hold those two next to any tokenized total and the proportion is the story.

Franklin Templeton is more direct. Its page states that "BENJI is the World's First U.S.-Registered Money Market Fund, Onchain", with one BENJI token representing one share of the Franklin OnChain U.S. Government Money Fund (FOBXX), across Stellar, Polygon, Arbitrum, Avalanche, Aptos, Ethereum, Base, Solana and BNB Smart Chain. The same page carries the sentence that defines what the blockchain is actually doing:

"The Fund's transfer agent maintains the official record of share ownership via a proprietary blockchain-integrated system, known as the Benji platform, that currently utilizes public blockchain networks for transaction activity."

The record is the transfer agent's. The chain carries transaction activity. That distinction, and what it means for a tokenized share, is the subject of an earlier brief on the Nasdaq tokenization rule, and it has not changed.

JPMorgan frames it as defence. Its Kinexys page, dated August 5, 2026, reports "~$7 billion transacted daily on the platform and over $4 trillion processed since inception", names JPM Coin on Base and the MONY and JLTXX funds, and quotes the unit's founder Umar Farooq: "If blockchain is going to disrupt traditional finance, we should own the disruption, rather than be disrupted by others."

Then there is Citadel Securities, which Kaleb asked about specifically, and which turns out to be the most interesting name in the set because it is arguing the other way. On December 2, 2025 it wrote to the SEC on tokenized U.S. equity securities and DeFi trading protocols. The letter is signed by Stephen John Berger, Managing Director and Global Head of Government & Regulatory Policy. It welcomes tokenization, then asks the Commission to "refrain from granting broad exemptive relief from the longstanding statutory definitions of an 'exchange' and 'broker-dealer'" and to proceed instead by notice-and-comment rulemaking.

Its reasoning is narrow and worth quoting rather than characterising. Broad relief "would create two separate regulatory regimes for the trading of the same security", an outcome the letter calls "the exact opposite of the 'technology-neutral' approach taken by the Exchange Act", and it warns against "facilitating the emergence of a 'shadow' U.S. equity market". Its principle is one sentence: "tokenized securities must succeed on the merits, rather than via regulatory exemptions."

Which is a pointed thing to have on file while the funds above are being distributed under Rule 506(b) and section 3(c)(7).

Who moved the money

The honest version of this list is shorter than the coverage suggests, and the chains are the least of it.

BlackRock publishes the contract addresses for BUIDL on nine networks: BNB Chain, Tempo, Ethereum, Solana, Polygon, Avalanche, Optimism, Arbitrum and Aptos. Where it publishes them is the detail. They sit on the firm's scams and fraud page, under this instruction:

"It is crucial to verify each character of the token addresses and ensure they match the official addresses. Fraudsters may create fake tokens and name them after genuine tokens to mislead victims."

That is the practical texture of institutional tokenization in 2026. The largest asset manager in the world distributes a cash fund across nine public blockchains, and the operational consequence is that it has to run an anti-impersonation registry and publish a character-by-character verification warning next to it.

What the roster adds up to, using only figures the institutions published themselves: Franklin Templeton on nine networks, BlackRock on nine, JPMorgan reporting about $7 billion a day through Kinexys, and BlackRock's own cash platform at nearly $1.073 trillion as of March 2026 against more than $8.4 trillion in U.S. money market funds as of May 2026.

So the money that moved on chain is real, it is concentrated in a handful of named funds from a handful of named firms, and the terms of access are set by securities law rather than by the ledger. The Form D is the clearest statement of that available. Nine blockchains on the distribution side, and on the subscription side a five million dollar minimum and no non-accredited investors.

Tokenization changed where the record of a transaction sits. It did not change who is allowed to be in the fund. For anyone tracking this because they expect it to reach ordinary business banking, the thing to watch is not the fund launches. It is the settlement networks named by The Clearing House and DTCC, and the two federal rulemakings whose comment files were still nearly empty five weeks out.

Market context

RWA.xyz tracks tokenized Treasury products across issuers and networks. Its tokenized Treasuries dashboard reported a Distributed Value of roughly $14.95 billion across 109 assets when read on October 8, 2026, alongside a separately labelled Represented Value of about $89.26 million. Those two totals measure different things and are not interchangeable. Set against the more than $8.4 trillion in U.S. money market funds that BlackRock reported for May 2026, tokenized Treasuries remain a small and early market. The chain is public. The offering is not.

The announcements describe access and the filings describe eligibility, and only one of those is legally binding. BlackRock, Franklin Templeton and JPMorgan have put real cash products on public blockchains, and the exemptions underneath the institutional versions are private placements limited to qualified purchasers, one of them with a five million dollar minimum and no sales yet reported. Citadel Securities, the largest market maker in the country, has asked the SEC in writing not to extend broad exemptive relief to tokenized equities on the grounds that technology should not earn a second rulebook. Tokenization has moved the ledger. It has not moved the gate.

Why Kansas Prairie Webworks is paying attention

I read Form D filings for the same reason I read cloture motions and comment dockets. They are free, they are public, and they are the version a firm has to tell a regulator rather than the version it tells a press release. On this one the two versions point in different directions, and the filing is four fields long.

For a business here, the useful read is about timing rather than access. None of these funds are open to you, and that is not a gap in your setup. What is worth watching is the plumbing underneath, because payment and settlement rails reach ordinary accounts years before the instruments do. When a processor, bank or platform tells you tokenized settlement is ready, the questions that work are the dull ones: which network, who is the transfer agent or recordkeeper, what exemption is the product sold under, and what is the minimum. Each of those has a documented answer, and a vendor who cannot give you all four is describing a roadmap.

The same discipline applies to anything built on top of it. If you are weighing a custom web app or an automation that touches payments or records, the question is not whether the technology is impressive. It is whether the thing it depends on is live, and who is accountable for the record when it is not.

Questions people are asking

What is a Form D, and why does it matter here?

A Form D is the notice an issuer files with the SEC when it sells securities without registering them for public sale, relying on an exemption instead. It is short and largely factual: the exemptions claimed, the offering size, the minimum investment, whether any non-accredited investors are involved, and how much has been sold. For tokenized funds it matters because it records the terms of access in a document the issuer is accountable for, which is often narrower than the terms implied by the product's public description.

Does a tokenized fund on a public blockchain mean anyone can buy it?

Not by itself. The network a token is issued on and the exemption the fund is sold under are separate questions. A fund can be distributed across nine public blockchains and still be limited by its filing to qualified purchasers with a multi-million dollar minimum, which is what the October 6, 2026 Form D for Franklin Onchain Institutional Liquidity Fund Ltd. records. Some tokenized funds are broadly available and some are not, and the filing is where that is settled rather than the chain.

What is the difference between a qualified purchaser and an accredited investor?

They are two different statutory thresholds, and qualified purchaser is the higher one. Accredited investor status under Regulation D can be met on income or net worth tests. The qualified purchaser standard governs the Investment Company Act section 3(c)(7) exclusion quoted in this brief and is defined separately in that Act. The Form D discussed here claims 3(c)(7) and separately records that it accepts no non-accredited investors.

Is Citadel Securities opposed to tokenization?

Its letter does not say that. It describes tokenization as an innovation with potential benefits for clearing and settlement efficiency, shareholder engagement and investor choice. What it opposes is a specific regulatory route: granting broad exemptive relief from the Exchange Act definitions of exchange and broker-dealer, which it argues would create two regulatory regimes for the same security. It asks for notice-and-comment rulemaking instead. The distinction between opposing a technology and opposing an exemption is the whole substance of the letter.

Why does this brief not give a growth figure for the tokenized Treasury market?

Because the comparison could not be made honestly at the time of writing. A widely repeated mid-August 2026 figure traces back to third-party summaries of the RWA.xyz dashboard rather than to the dashboard itself, and the dashboard reports two different totals, Distributed Value and Represented Value, which differ by more than two orders of magnitude. Without knowing which total a secondary summary measured, subtracting one number from the other would produce a trend that is not in either source. The single dated reading is reported instead, with its own caveats.
